Vocab A · pp. 150–161 (Sunday)

Term (page)MeaningMemory hook
Gridlock (150)Congress can’t pass laws because of party fighting (inside Congress or with the president).Traffic jam: nobody moves.
Universalism (151)Norm: spread federal money to as many districts and states as possible.Everybody gets a slice.
Logrolling (151)Trading votes: I vote for your bill, you vote for mine.“You scratch my back, I’ll scratch yours.”
Earmarks (151)Federally funded local projects attached to bills. Called pork-barrel spending.A tag on the bill: “this $ goes to MY town.”
Specialization (152)Norm: become an expert on a few issues. Stronger in the House; senators are generalists.House = specialists, Senate = generalists.
Seniority (152)Norm: the longest-serving member on a committee usually becomes chair.Longest on the team = captain. A norm, not a rule.
Speaker of the House (152)Elected leader of the House. The only House leader named in the Constitution. All House members vote for the Speaker.The Speaker speaks for the whole House.
Majority Leader (154)Head of the party with the most seats in the House or Senate; runs day-to-day work. In the Senate, controls what reaches the floor.Most seats = most say.
Minority Leader (154)Head of the party with fewer seats.Leader of the smaller team.
Whip system (154)House party leaders who 1) gather information, 2) spread information, 3) build coalitions. They count votes and persuade.Fox hunt “whipper-in” keeps the hounds with the pack.
President pro tempore (154)Symbolic Senate job, usually the most senior majority senator.“Pro tem” = for the time being. No real power.
Roll call vote (156)A recorded vote: yes, no, present, or abstain.Like roll call in class: your name is on it.
Party vote (156)Most of one party votes against most of the other.Team vs. team.
Party unity (156)How often members of the same party vote together on party votes.Teammates sticking together.
Conditional party government (157)Party leaders are strong only if members agree to follow, which is likelier when the parties are very different and united inside.Strong leader on one condition: the team agrees.
Standing committees (158)Permanent committees that do most of the work: write bills, oversee laws. 21 in the House, 20 in the Senate.Standing = always there.
Select committees (158)Handle one specific issue for one or two terms.Selected for one job.
Joint committees (158)Members of both chambers; limited power.Joint = House + Senate together.
Conference committees (159)Temporary; work out differences when the House and Senate pass different versions of a bill.A conference to settle the argument.
Distributive theory (159)Members pick committees that help their district (farm-state members on Agriculture) and back each other’s bills.Distribute goodies back home.
Informational theory (159)Committees full of experts help everyone make well-informed decisions.Committees = information.
House Rules Committee (160, 168)Sets how long a bill is debated and which amendments are allowed; an arm of the majority leaders. Majority holds 9 of 13 seats.Rules Committee = the referee who sets the game rules.
Unorthodox lawmaking (160)Barbara Sinclair’s term: many important laws skip the neat textbook steps.Un-orthodox = not by the book.

Term (page)MeaningMemory hook
Multiple referral (164)In the House, a big bill goes to more than one committee.Referred to many.

Rider (167)An extra item attached to a must-pass bill.It rides along for free.

Oversight (169)Congress checks that the bureaucracy carries out laws the way Congress intended.Over-see = watch over.
Power of the purse (169)Congress’s power to raise and spend money; it can cut an agency’s funding.Whoever holds the wallet has power.

Shell bill (173)An existing House bill emptied and rewritten (the CARES Act used H.R. 748) so a money bill “starts” in the House.Empty shell, new filling.

3Top 10 Big Ideas

1 · The dilemma (p. 150)

People like their own member but dislike Congress. Members have 2 jobs that clash: responsibility (good policy for the nation) vs. responsiveness (deliver for home). Voters want lower taxes, more spending, and a balanced budget, all at once. Members pick home, so Congress looks stuck.

Remember: home beats nation.

2 · Structure (pp. 150–152)

The reelection goal shapes how Congress is built. Formal: staff, committees, parties, leaders. Informal: norms = universalism, logrolling, specialization, seniority.

Remember: formal = official, norms = unwritten.

3 · Party leaders (pp. 152–154)

House: Speaker (all members vote; only leader in the Constitution), Majority Leader, whip. Senate: Majority Leader, whip, president pro tempore; the vice president is president of the Senate but only shows up to break a tie. Senate leaders are weaker because senators have unlimited debate.

Remember: House leaders strong, Senate leaders weak.

4 · Parties got stronger (pp. 155–157)

U.S. parties are weak compared with parliamentary systems, but much stronger since the 1960s. About 70% of roll call votes are party votes; in 2019, 95% of House Democrats voted together. Why: Southern Democrats now vote like northern Democrats, and moderate Republicans mostly disappeared. Most polarized ever (Figure 4.6).

Remember: no middle left.

5 · How leaders lead (pp. 157–158)

Leaders can’t give orders. They persuade, control the agenda (friendly bills come up, party-splitting votes don’t), and do favors: campaign visits, fund raising, good committee seats. Obama = “Fundraiser in Chief” (2014). Reelection always beats party. The Freedom Caucus pushed out Speaker Boehner (2015); the Squad pressured Pelosi.

Remember: persuade, don’t order.

6 · Committees (pp. 158–160)

4 types: standing, select, joint, conference. Two theories: distributive (help my district) and informational (expertise). The majority gives itself extra seats on key committees (Ways and Means, Appropriations, Rules). Staff grew in the 1970s–80s. Congress is the creation of its members, so they can change it.

Remember: S-S-J-C.

7 · How a bill becomes a law (pp. 160–165)

The one rule that never changes: pass both chambers in identical form, then the president signs. Only members can introduce bills (House: the hopper; even the president needs a member). 80–90% of bills die in committee or subcommittee. House floor: tight time limits; Senate: usually unlimited debate. Votes: voice, division (stand), recorded (25 members ask).

Remember: same bill, both chambers, president.

8 · Shortcuts (pp. 166–167)

Many big bills skip steps: 1) bypass committees (up to 20%; discharge petition or special rule; Patriot Act after 9/11), 2) changed after committee (about 1/3; the ACA), 3) summits (president + leaders make a deal), 4) omnibus bills with riders.

Remember: bypass, change, summit, omnibus.

9 · House vs. Senate (pp. 167–168)

House = majoritarian (since Reed’s Rules, 1890): the majority almost always wins; Rules Committee writes a special rule for each major bill. Senate = individualistic (“herding cats”): filibuster, cloture (60), unanimous consent, holds, reconciliation (51). 2013: Democrats ended filibusters for most presidential nominees; 2017: Republicans did the same for Supreme Court nominees. 2/3 of senators carry over, so Senate rules stay stable; the House re-adopts its rules every session.

Remember: House = team, Senate = individuals.

10 · Oversight + CARES Act (pp. 169–173)

After a law passes, Congress watches the bureaucracy: power of the purse (blunt, rarely used), hearings and investigations (common), advice and consent, impeachment. Impeached: Andrew Johnson, Bill Clinton, Donald Trump; none removed. 2020: Supreme Court ruled 7–2 against Trump’s claim of total immunity from congressional subpoenas. COVID: the White House refused to let officials testify; 200+ House letters were rejected or ignored.

Remember: pass it, then watch it.

The 8 steps (p. 161)

  1. A member introduces the bill.
  2. Committees and subcommittees craft it (hearings, witnesses, markup).
  3. It passes the floor of one chamber.
  4. Committee and floor action in the other chamber.
  5. A conference committee works out differences (minor bills: one chamber accepts the other’s version, or amendments go back and forth).
  6. Both chambers approve the final version.
  7. The president signs or vetoes it.
  8. Congress can override a veto with 2/3 of both chambers.

Trap: the House path has the Rules Committee; the Senate path does not (the Majority Leader controls the floor). A committee can pass, change, or table (kill) a bill. Its report becomes part of the legislative history courts and agencies use later.

Real example: the 2017 Tax Cuts and Jobs Act (p. 163)

  • All tax bills must start in the House, so House Ways and Means wrote H.R. 1; the House passed it.
  • Senate Republicans lacked the 60 votes to stop a filibuster, so the Senate Budget Committee made it a reconciliation bill (51 votes). It kept the House title.
  • A vote-a-rama: 355 amendments in an overnight marathon. Deals with holdouts + conference committee → both chambers passed it → President Trump signed it.

AP Policy in Practice: the CARES Act (pp. 171–173)

Policy stageWhat happened
BackgroundFederalism: each state handled COVID differently; the Trump administration left most decisions to the states. France (unitary government): national leaders decided for the whole country. Unemployment not seen since the Great Depression. CARES was the 3rd COVID bill and the first only about the economy.
1. Problem recognitionA pandemic, rising unemployment, overloaded hospitals.
2. Agenda settingPhase 1: $8.3 billion for supplies and vaccines. Phase 2: the Families First Act (free testing, paid leave). Andrew Yang’s universal basic income idea ($1,000/month) came back into the news.
3. DeliberationRevenue bills must start in the House, so the Senate used a shell bill (H.R. 748). Republicans wanted more for businesses; Democrats wanted more for workers.
4. EnactmentSenate passed it 96–0 on March 25, 2020; House agreed by voice vote; Trump signed March 27.
5. ImplementationIndividuals got 30% ($1,200 per adult, $500 per child); big business 25%; small business 19%.

Economists disagree: supply side (businesses, linked to the right) vs. demand side / Keynesian (people, linked to the left). The CARES Act was a middle ground. Bismarck: laws are like sausages, better not to see them made. Knowing the steps and veto points helps you judge complaints about gridlock.

7One-Page Cheat Sheet (last 5 minutes)

The big picture

  • Home beats nation (dilemma).
  • Reelection shapes everything.
  • Norms: universalism, logrolling, specialization, seniority.
  • Parties: weak vs. other countries, but strongest ever; ~70% party votes.

Leaders

  • Speaker = only one in Constitution, all vote.
  • Whip: gather, spread, build.
  • Pro tem = symbolic. VP breaks ties.
  • Conditional party government: strong only if members follow.

Committees

  • Standing (permanent: 21 House / 20 Senate), select, joint, conference.
  • Distributive vs. informational.
  • 80–90% of bills die here.
  • Markup = final wording.

Bill → law

  • Same bill, both chambers, president.
  • House: hopper, Rules Committee (closed/open/modified).
  • Senate: filibuster, cloture 60, reconciliation 51, unanimous consent, holds.
  • Veto override 2/3 both. Pocket veto 10 days.

Shortcuts

  • Bypass committees (~20%; discharge petition).
  • Changed after committee (~1/3; ACA).
  • Summits. Omnibus + riders.
  • 2017 tax law: reconciliation + vote-a-rama.

Oversight + CARES

  • Power of the purse (blunt), hearings (common).
  • Fire alarm (common) vs. police patrol.
  • House impeaches, Senate tries: Johnson, Clinton, Trump.
  • CARES: shell bill H.R. 748, 96–0, $1,200/adult.
